The first day of the Lunar New Year—wishing you a happy spring.

The third year of the pandemic brought many changes, yet for many people it was slightly more stable than the previous two. This is especially true for me—reading [last year’s year-end review](http://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzkyMjIwODAyMQ==&mid=2247483673&idx=1&sn=cce140431ef624224d4cd5bff7098033&chksm=c1f69363f6811a7503993e725c82466323e69ed4a830de88fad34eb9fb79da31f0b049706dc4&scene=21#wechat_redirect) and [my job-search notes](http://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzkyMjIwODAyMQ==&mid=2247483674&idx=1&sn=65b7ac745ac855fced361307411ebc4f&chksm=c1f69360f6811a76b0d17c16d3039cd9d365dc403d0423632318f5d4275bf04486b2b7528aec&scene=21#wechat_redirect), my thinking on the big picture is basically unchanged. Although I ran into quite a few new problems in actually carrying things out, compared with the previous two years, against the backdrop of moving from abroad to China and from finance to the internet, adjusting to a new environment and landing there, I reached this year’s main goals as expected. This review skips the big framework and mainly records some specific (and scattered) reflections.

## Work

### The Job Itself

It will soon be a full year since I joined in March. Although there were many internal and external changes in between, and my team changed a lot—the large team grew severalfold, and my small team split off more independently from the structure, expanding from two people to over a dozen—in broad strokes, in terms of results, I went from being completely new to the field to independently owning a direction and being able to guide others’ and the team’s work, which counts as basically competent. In terms of the content of work, my role has become “softer”: the main form of output has changed from code to documents, and I often need to coordinate and organize different people; there have also been some opportunities to initiate projects and propose innovative solutions. In terms of the larger environment, although there were all kinds of changes during the year, I am indeed working at a company that is outstanding in culture, management and many other respects; its resources, people and projects offer enough to learn from and practice on. Compared with a year ago, the growth in my soft skills and experience is visible. Whichever company in the industry I work for from here, I am confident I can do the job efficiently (“Work Habits,” ways of thinking, and so on). From these angles, the original purpose of choosing this job when I returned to China has largely been achieved.

On the business side, computational advertising is a business where machine learning was applied early, which is mature and consistent with the recommendation algorithms the company is best at, and the DS role has a broad perspective (the downside being that the work is scattered), so my knowledge of newer developments and models in machine learning, and my concrete experience applying them in engineering (which is very important), have grown considerably. Although I am still feeling my way, my past experience was basically related to the application of data methods and artificial intelligence, which in a broad sense can be seen as two very different application scenarios of the same kind of technology. Through practice and comparison, I seem to have gotten a clearer sense of the boundaries and logic of this area. Of course my current depth and breadth are far from enough, and I need to keep learning.

Of course, there are also many things I did not do well and many obstacles to moving forward. First, what I am doing now connects with too many directions with too few resources, making it hard to find the energy for projects with greater depth and innovation; my perspective on things is still too low and too fine-grained, and in a relatively mature business, it is often still new methods solving old problems. Second, although I am technically fairly proficient within the group, I lack deep thinking (for example, horizontally: from the problem we want to solve to the product architecture, to the technical architecture, to the data pipeline; how we do many things and how competitors do them; vertically: thinking from the perspective of leaders and collaborators, how different people are organized, and so on). Changing the external environment and opportunities is one way to address this, but **speeding up my own progress, so as not to be shaped by the environment, matters more**.

### On the Larger Environment and the Industry

This year’s changes in the internet—from large-platform regulation and even changes in the overall logic, to market traffic peaking, to pressure on verticals like gaming and education—are plain from a glance at the stock market and the various reports, and need no elaboration here. There are many questions worth studying here (they could be written up separately), but what is more relevant to personal development is not what is changing or will change, but “what does not change?” As Bezos has said, companies, investors and decision-makers all care more about what will change, and business and investment decisions do have to pursue short-term adaptation (for example, this year’s education sector, the rise of Xiaohongshu, Ant Group, CATL and so on). But **an individual really cannot (and should not) pick the fast horse; the starting point of strategy can only rely on things that are relatively stable**—for example, many of today’s hot machine learning scholars began and deepened their research during the “winter.”

On the internet, in conversations with quite a few friends this year (practitioners, and finance professionals in primary and secondary markets), I found views quite polarized: after the end of “disorderly expansion,” some believe internet companies will become platforms and infrastructure; some believe the pressure is temporary and wait for the next wave of innovation to reshuffle the deck; and there are all sorts of in-between and more extreme views.

In fact, **how these changes happen and how they will affect the work I currently do are very hard to predict** (for example, this year’s stagnation in advertising revenue). Then, for an individual, is a better logic one of footholds like “*people who master relatively advanced tools and ways of collaborating still have scarcity value*,” “*only specialized, refined and distinctive talent has scarcity value*,” or “*ability + circle + distinctiveness determines your influence*”? Having a foothold, how to translate it into a concrete road is harder still—like knowing that in the end the whole world will drive electric vehicles but having to choose between buying a Tesla or Google, or knowing that digital currency is ultimately the future but having to pick one out of fifteen thousand. In life it is easy to talk principles and enormously hard to get things done, and in real career development there is more of luck and path dependence.

Even so, **constantly thinking about the external environment and one’s own choices, and acting courageously, is still meaningful**—because between choosing and being chosen, most people still prefer to be chosen. And doing what most people do while expecting a different result is the easiest mistake to make.

Standing in the present, rather than thinking about how the whole industry or even the business will fare, I worry more about whether my own growth will slow down markedly as growth decelerates (the head effect of a power-law distribution depends on the number of individuals joining the network and the speed at which the network expands). And the internal factors I can control may actually have more room for improvement than the external environment, so **doing myself well comes first**.

## Learning

In terms of input, the total amount of reading and study in 2021 was more than in the past (partly because I was doing new things), basically one to two books a week. Yet my biggest realization about learning was that reading books may not be of much use (though this should not itself be too utilitarian), for two main reasons.

First, from practical experience, ability and knowledge do not seem to have much to do with each other. Rather than the absolute amount of knowledge, the people I admire at work can, given the same information and background, **use logic more to analyze information, have a stronger spirit of doubt and self-examination, and pay more attention to detail**. Thinking about it carefully, sometimes knowing too many scattered things without being able to integrate them is actually bad for these qualities.

Second, from the standpoint of growing insight, knowledge related to solving practical problems is mastered more deeply (e.g., Edgar Dale’s Cone of Learning). For example, many books on business models and business cases left a deep impression and gave me insights only when I reread them this year, while I forgot quite a bit of the specific knowledge of finance. Like a soldier learning to shoot, **whether or not you take part in a war, whether there are people who have really fought and won battles around you, makes an absolutely different effect**. Rather than organizing my knowledge system by reading lists, it is more valuable to start from practical problems I have paid attention to and solved. After all, in the information age, what is scarce is not information but ways of processing and integrating it effectively (ones that land on solutions, keep up with change, link pieces of knowledge, and communicate views effectively).

So as for what I gained most this year, specific knowledge is actually negligible; I can only say that, first, my understanding of which parts of my knowledge system are valuable and need to go deeper became more concrete; and second, I gained more firsthand experience of the boundaries and limitations of individuals and organizations.

Specifically, many things are easier than I had imagined (though still hard): making a successful product, expanding quickly in a new business, achieving results on some concrete matters, becoming a business expert in some area—none of these is a technique without traces to follow; while many **seemingly simple things are much harder to do than imagined**: for example, repeating and polishing simple actions (such as the many simple actions in leading people, communicating and writing), doing thinking and analysis that has no short-term payoff (thinking a few more steps ahead), admitting and summarizing my own failed experience and attempts, and admitting my limitations and seeking resources and help. There was also a lot of wasted effort: for example, I often made plans, but in fact short-term plans and long-term goals can still serve some purpose, while mid-term plans (six months or more) often brought interference and procrastination; also, too many plans can turn into tactical diligence that harms the ability to act. This is true not only for individuals but also for organizations and even industries; many especially smart people do not ask silly questions or do simple things, which is what leaves opportunities for the less smart. And as long as one keeps working for a while in a direction where one has passion and is grounded, there are still plenty of chances to produce results.

In 2022, my biggest hope for learning is **more of an ordinary mind**—make good use of the information environment around me, pursue information that has a barrier to entry rather than chasing trending topics, and at the same time summarize and produce more views from simple to deep, without being bound by form. Talk with people more, combining inward reflection and accumulation with outward search for opportunities. As for my shortcomings, be less perfectionist and seek help and collaboration from others more. “Only with an ordinary mind can one do extraordinary things.”

## Life

Life is where I made clearer progress than in previous years, and I also put more attention and energy into it than before.

First, I got along a bit better with myself. As said above, discovering and admitting my own shortcomings and limitations is not easy; along the way one has to learn to coexist with one’s emotions, to give oneself care, and to undertake a long and honest process of self-discovery. Only on the basis of making peace with oneself can one keep revising one’s values, design a map of life, and gradually take on more responsibility.

Second, in the half year since I settled in Shanghai, I was fairly proactive in social life. Whether looking for friends or for a suitable partner, one has to go through the process of bravely stepping out. This year I took the initiative to join quite a few events for meeting new friends, and also contacted many old classmates and friends in Shanghai. Only by really interacting with people can one see and think clearly about the influence different people have on oneself, what kind of people one should associate with, how to regard the different traits of different people, and so on. More importantly, **the roles I play in various relationships also keep shaping and influencing me**—what kind of person I am and what kind of person I want to become often can only be seen through others as a mirror. In a word, the specific problems of life can only be figured out by walking into life.

With more understanding of myself and of life, I also understand happiness a bit more. One insight is that **so-called happiness is largely brought by relatively immediate joy or pleasure (Joy) together with a sense of meaning (Meaning)**. Observing myself and those around me, whatever one’s personality and circumstances, whether one’s sense of happiness is high or low can mostly be attributed using this framework. For me, I had been spending a bit too much of my time rushing after relatively long-term goals and progress, always thinking of learning a bit more and reaching a bit higher, and paid less attention to joy in the present. In fact, being calmer, having some joys within easy reach every day (music, coffee, good food, loved ones, and of course as little Douyin-style “tit-tainment” as possible), and attending to and experiencing the present with care (“mindfulness”) may be the right way to make time more worthwhile. Yesterday, while putting up the New Year couplets, I thought of “make wishes of the highest grade, form ties of the middle grade, enjoy blessings of the lowest grade,” and I hope to put it well into practice.

Finally, I quote two lines from the 2021 Yale opening address that moved me: “**Before we change the world, we need to change ourselves; before we lead the world, we must first learn to lead ourselves**” (https://president.yale.edu/president/speeches/when-world-fire). I think I should also view my development and future changes under the guidance of this line. May everyone find their own way of leading themselves in the new year, and do better and better on a road that suits them.
