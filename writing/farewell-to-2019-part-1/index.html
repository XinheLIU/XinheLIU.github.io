Farewell to 2019 (Part 1)

2025-10-15 · 14 min read Personal Growth

Today is Christmas 2019. In the West it is a day when families gather and welcome the new year, and the start of saying goodbye to 2019 and stepping into 2020. During this time people spend more time with loved ones, reach out to friends, be with the people who matter, look back on the past and look ahead to the year to come. So, following “international custom,” I am using this time to review my past year briefly, to be grateful for what I gained, and to look ahead and work harder on the areas where I did poorly.

2019 was my second year at work, and a year of considerable change in my life, with many ups and downs. I have sorted the things I want to talk about into good and bad. Today I will cover what I accomplished and did well; the next two pieces will cover what I did not do well and what I hope to do in the coming year.

The thing I think I did most successfully this year was to build a few good habits.

Habits are nothing but small things. But compared with the many muddled “achievements” of the past, which felt important at the time and, once the moment passed, often left no lasting effect, these day-to-day small things strike me more deeply now.

- Rising early, exercising, and eating by plan

Rising early came about by happenstance. At the end of April this year I broke up with my girlfriend of six years and went through a full week of insomnia. I gradually found that if I woke up very early every day, I would naturally get sleepy at night and my sleep improved, so I simply made myself get up at 5:30 every day and then read or exercise. This did not actually make me sleep less or more, and I did not have the profound experience others describe of “the morning is the time to plan the day”—that mornings are decidedly more efficient than evenings—but it really did improve my quality of life. I think there are two main reasons. First, because I have to make an effort to win those two hours in the morning, I cherish them more, and knowing my evenings will be limited, I find myself wasting a little less time without noticing. Second, even a small thing, if kept up, brings confidence and a sense of accomplishment, and the effect of confidence is long-lasting and powerful.

Likewise, exercise and planning meals ahead helped a great deal with my recovery at the time. I was able to carry them out effectively thanks to last year’s experience of losing some weight by a scientific method, introduced to me by a colleague. For one thing, that experience gave me a deeper appreciation that doing things takes a scientific method, patience with oneself, and gradual improvement—“small steps, fast pace.” For another, I came to see more clearly how physical condition affects one’s mental and emotional state. Whatever difficulties come up, keeping up physical health and a positive way of living is the foundation for continuing to move forward.

- Systematic learning

Learning is a big topic that I will keep unpacking in later articles. Over the past year, after many stumbling attempts, from September onward I was basically able to study in a targeted way at fixed times and in fragments of spare time. The two points that struck me most in the process were these:

First, I came to recognize more clearly the importance of systematic learning, and it corrected many errors in my earlier understanding and methods.

Basically, I am someone with a fairly strong interest in learning and understanding new things, and learning something new brings me great joy. In daily life, compared with peers in my circle, I am the type with a wider range of knowledge. But more experience and deeper knowledge in various areas have made me realize more deeply that for knowledge to actually work and get things done, one must have the right way of learning. By “right” I mean that the standard is being able to apply what one learns. Achieving this involves finding objective, high-quality learning resources; building a correct, complete knowledge system that suits oneself; dull, deliberate practice; constantly summarizing and correcting oneself; and continually coming into contact with useful information from outside one’s circle.

For an individual, this is not easy in many respects and has to be done deliberately. Moreover, it differs quite a bit from learning at school, which has an end and has answers; it requires constantly raising one’s existing level of understanding, and thinking and asking more. The ancient Chinese said, “Those who labor with their minds govern others; those who labor with their strength are governed by others.” I think that in my earlier schooling and exam-taking, and in learning technology on the job, most of it was labor of the “strength” kind, which is easier compared with more effective learning.

Second, I recognized the importance of doing fewer things and being selective. I feel strongly about this in other areas too; today I will start with learning.

The information age easily gives people an illusion: knowledge is within easy reach, and as long as you have time you can always learn something and make progress. But what is exploding is information, not necessarily knowledge. On the contrary, precisely because information is exploding, everyone can publish their own views, and people tend to be surrounded by the knowledge and opinions they are used to accepting. This means that without deliberate effort, ordinary people find it harder to obtain knowledge and viewpoints beyond their own circle, environment and platform; their field of view concentrates and deepens in the domains their work touches and in the information and hobbies they follow online. Meanwhile, for learning the knowledge and skills one truly needs over the long term, time is increasingly limited while the available information sources multiply exponentially. This calls for deliberately choosing one’s information sources, learning methods and horizons, and for more initiative in learning. Also, living in a society that is changing faster than ever and is full of uncertainty, it is no longer possible to master once-and-for-all big principles and hard skills, so learning how to learn—how to filter, select, accept and process information and knowledge—becomes ever more critical.

- Picking up the reading habit again

Following on from this, when choosing information, being able to settle down and read books, especially classics—a source whose useful life is longer than that of information feeds—is often a good way to set oneself apart from peers. But the benefits of reading go far beyond that. People who know how to enjoy reading not only get to touch a larger world but also understand themselves more fully. As the sayings go, reading “cultivates the temperament,” and “read useless books, be an interesting person”; spending some time on reading really does make one feel an improvement in the quality of life. Since the beginning of the year I have averaged at least ten pages a day, more on weekends, and setting aside time to be with books helped me a lot during hard and confusing periods.

In reading, what influenced me most last year were several columns by Dr. Wu Jun, which spelled out many truths I had felt in life but lacked pointers for, and influenced me in all kinds of ways. Beyond the most important influence—how to look at problems and approach things—they also led me to do some things I had not been in a hurry to do before. Picking up books I had read and hobbies I had had, which added much interest to life; actively going out to walk and see, with two trips this year-end to the American West and to Iceland; and starting to write now—all of these can be said to be related. And much of what I write is simply my own experience, in my specific life, of some of the big principles he speaks of.

The second important thing is that my mindset improved a lot over the past year.

“Better” here has a subjective definition for each person. Here I mainly mean that I look at problems more objectively than before, can analyze them from more and broader perspectives, and face problems with more calm. This mainly stems from the habits and the growth in experience mentioned above.

At work, the biggest change in my understanding was recognizing boundaries. By boundaries I mean the limits of how things are done—the boundaries within which I myself, my team and the company operate, even the boundaries of change in the wider environment. I started out restless, unsure of the way, with high aims but low skill; now I have completed a relatively independent system and handle known and unknown daily problems. What I have most come to appreciate about method through the bumps is the importance of all kinds of boundaries, and how to recognize and expand existing ones. I will expand on this in later articles.

For myself personally, it means seeing where a small system sits within the larger system, seeing the strengths and weaknesses of each person on the team and of the team itself, and recognizing how hard it is to make change—every change comes from many small changes, and these deserve enough patience, persistence and effort. “Knowing it cannot be done yet doing it” often hurts efficiency. As for my own views, they have to be examined and studied again and again; often a seemingly imperfect solution has been tested by time, and a good idea you can think of is often one that others have thought of and tried without results. So the more I work, the more deeply I feel that “knowing is easy, doing is hard”—pointing out shortcomings is easy, while making change is often hard. The truly effective approach is to make better use of the resources at hand, do less futile work, put resources where they matter, and, once you have decided to do something, find ways to do it to the extreme. Both are hard. The way to do them is to understand how to recognize boundaries, use boundaries, and do good work within them. Knowing these principles, when facing problems and friction in work, one can often see wider and farther, and look at the problem at hand with more calm.

In life, two things helped my attitude a great deal, and both can be expanded on later. One is spending more time on my relationship with my parents. The seven or eight years from university until now, the time in which one steps out of the family, are often also the time of separating from one’s parents into an independent household and individual, and this is especially pronounced for me. Compared with many friends around me, I think I am among the Chinese kids who from childhood made more of their own decisions and gave their parents less to worry about. So after university I went home rarely and never had deep conversations with my parents. In the second half of 2019 I spent a lot of time on this, talking with my parents as deeply as possible and letting them mature along with me; although many shortcomings remain, this is something I can affirm in myself. The other is my understanding of romantic relationships, and of interpersonal relationships more generally. There is much to unpack here; I will only emphasize the importance of initiative and practice. I think it is like this with many things in life—some truths can only be learned by dealing with people and putting them into practice yourself.

The third thing is that my level and efficiency in getting things done improved.

Beyond progress in all aspects of work this year, I also learned some new courses and skills outside work. Of the ones I completed, the deepest takeaways are as follows:

  • First, the importance of measurement and repetition in doing things and in learning. This belongs to the systematic learning mentioned above; I single it out because it applies not only to learning but to doing anything. Professionals whose learning and work are mostly programming often have a deep feel for “practice makes perfect”—gains in efficiency per unit of time often come from dull repetition of basic moves and the highest-frequency operations. And objective measurement acts as a catalyst for the efficiency of repeated practice.
  • Second, the importance of expression. Being able to articulate what you have learned is not easy. I felt this more at work, while studying algorithms, and while preparing for the CFA exam this year. But once you express it, it brings immediate improvement for yourself.
  • Third, doing things often takes more time than imagined, even fifty to a hundred percent more. I believe anyone who has worked knows this well, but I want to stress that beyond dependence on collaborating with others, there is a large element of overestimating one’s own ability. An undeniable fact is that, within the same order of magnitude, for most things, a person of outstanding ability doing thirty or forty percent better than average is already quite hard; overestimating what you can complete only leads to a strong start and a weak finish, and things not done well.
  • Fourth, the importance of confidence and courage. Often we lack a little reasonable courage to do what we should. This was my biggest takeaway from planning two trips at year-end, one of which was almost entirely to practice driving (I had just gotten my license).

Overall, I think the most cherishable gains of this year are the understanding and experience that, for me, can only be internalized through personal experience. If there is any value in them, I mainly have to thank the people and events I encountered. I think that in any environment, those who know how to cherish and be grateful, and keep a positive attitude toward life, will gain something.

December 25, 2019

In New York
