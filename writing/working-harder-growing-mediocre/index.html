The Efficient Road to No Where: Why Working Harder Can Keep You Mediocre

2026-10-11 · 19 min read Career Personal Growth

The Efficient Road to No Where: Why Working Harder Can Keep You Mediocre

Over the National Day holiday I went back to a course I bought five years ago. I listened to it once back then, and it all sounded like platitudes. Coming back to it in my early thirties, I realized none of it had sunk in the first time. As the old saying goes, I heard the tune but missed its meaning.

I’m writing this down in the hope that I’ll stop coasting.

Middlemarch

Everyone is full of ambition when young. Yet somehow most people get stuck along the way and settle into a comfortable, mediocre life they can’t pull themselves out of. They remain stranded halfway, forever. That is the story of George Eliot’s Middlemarch. The middle class is less an economic stratum than a state of mind. My twenty-year-old self would surely find my thirty-something self mediocre and lacking drive. The novel, of course, is about people in their twenties who don’t yet understand the world. But turn it around: stalling in your thirties is a very real gravitational pull, too.

Where does this gravity come from? From avoidance, from the way society divides labor, and from the education we received.

Most of our life experience, and the training systems we pass through, stem from the fact that humans are the most cooperative species on the planet, so we train people for social cooperation. And what is cooperation? At bottom, it’s letting other people solve your problems. Meet a certain standard, and schools, big companies and job titles will solve many of your problems for you: survival, respect, even the questions of “what should I do” and “why should I do it.” Big tech, the civil service, the fine-grained division of white-collar labor: all of them cultivate a refined comfort, an environment you can lean on. To some degree, they let you dodge the real problems.

You think you’re pursuing an ideal, but mostly you’re pursuing a story others told you: social approval, being around smart people, joining an exciting industry. Going to the US, doing quant, coming back to China to work in tech: those were my own choices, of course. But was there avoidance of choice, and a lack of confidence, behind them? Telling yourself that “who you travel with matters more than where you go” is, underneath, a fear of making the wrong call and of not being good enough. Calling it “incremental growth” to join an environment full of smart people, gradually outperform them, and then move on to the next one is a refusal to admit that what most people want is approval and comfort from their environment, not true knowledge. If you always look for answers alongside the majority, you may, in a sense, be dodging the harder questions.

I thought I was already fairly self-driven, but it’s really just a worker’s mindset in nicer packaging.

Familiar Knowledge and True Knowledge

Hegel wrote of “the difference between familiar knowledge and true knowledge.” Familiar knowledge is what you’re acquainted with; true knowledge is what you’ve genuinely mastered and can use to solve real problems. Getting from one to the other means constantly questioning, challenging, and stripping away false knowledge.

Why is familiar knowledge usually plentiful and true knowledge scarce? Because people are rarely confronted with real questions. Only in a real environment with selection pressure, where you’re forced to answer and get eliminated if you can’t come up with a solution, does a person have even a 50% chance of iterating and evolving their cognition.

But this cuts against human instinct, especially for people who lack security and are driven by anxiety. Take me: I’ve worked for many years, but how many real problems have I actually solved? The strategy work at Goldman Sachs had real depth; some of it could have been published as papers. But at its core it rested on an investment bank’s enormous balance sheet, which let it monopolize products only it could trade, and on a powerful sales force. My work at ByteDance looked fancy on the surface, but mostly someone else had built the stage and I was carving decorations on it. What came closer to real problem-solving was building from zero to one: staking out a patch of ground, growing a team, leading a group of people. That gave me some real understanding of both the work and the people. But above that level, especially the promotion game, it gets genuinely nauseating and boring. It’s all about how to frame things, how to write OKRs, how to look clear-headed, how to prove you’re smarter than everyone else. For the giant machine, this is a visible reduction of internal entropy. For the individual, it’s a skill that’s worthless outside the system. That’s why big tech’s middle managers are, in essence, a heavily domesticated and alienated group. In heavily shielded or monopolistic industries, the problems and environment you and your company face are quite narrow. The wider world may be fiercely competitive, but certain individual “skills” (call them skills or not) let large numbers of middle managers get by through cover-ups and fudging, using effort that isn’t real work to paper over gaps in understanding.

The biggest problem with staying on this track and in this environment for so long is that what you think you understand or can do is mostly familiar knowledge, not true knowledge. Set aside how much of most people’s work really just serves a few managers and administrative needs. Even the things I studied in depth, like “I studied quantitative finance,” “I understand how technology drives business,” “I understand internet business and management models,” even going to other companies to explain these to people, may all be “bird-flight school” understanding. You have to realize that a bird’s wings are not oars but sails. First-principles thinking must come either from direct practice in the market or from deep, focused thought. I’ve dodged both.

Single-Loop and Double-Loop Learning

So how do we solve this?

Einstein said: “Problems cannot be solved with the same thinking that created them.” You can’t lift yourself off the ground by pulling on your own hair, because there’s no fulcrum. If your thinking has no fulcrum, no point of leverage separate from your current way of thinking, much of your effort will achieve little.

Between my own avoidance and stagnation and the domesticating effect of my environment, most of my effort has probably gone into “single-loop learning”: repeatedly reinforcing an existing cognitive model. Such a model guides you, but it also confines your thinking. The end result: “when all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail.” As your cognition grows more complete, you quietly castrate your own capacity to think. On the surface, “understanding tech, data and business” looks like steadily accumulating experience. In reality, it may just be a fixed set of talking points, SOPs, and half-right moves.

Truly effective learning is “double-loop learning”: keep reinforcing your cognitive model within the existing framework, while also weakening, or even dismantling, that model.

How do you dismantle it?

“Cognition” means asking questions; “non-cognition” means nothing ever becomes a question.

Socrates said: “I am no wiser than anyone else. I know nothing, except for one thing: I know that I don’t know, while everyone else thinks they know.” The key to the right answer is figuring out what question the examiner is really asking. Until the right question exists, the answer doesn’t.

I may have been asking the wrong questions these past years. Not just “How can I do better?” but “What should I give up?” and “What am I doing, and why?”

Pick the right track. Nothing is sadder than doing the wrong thing very efficiently.

Quant and my years at ByteDance were highly efficient, but in big tech the real leverage lies in managing relationships. This year at Dongshi has also been busy and fulfilling. But if I’m still thinking only about “doing things well,” I’m still just optimizing inside a framework someone else defined.

What should I give up? Stop asking “what should I pay attention to,” and focus on “expanding my sphere of influence.” Society is a web of entanglements; you can’t fully break free of your family of origin or your surroundings. Relying only on outperforming others, on being excellent, on climbing relentlessly, is still the school mindset: the belief that effort alone solves problems. Problems don’t solve themselves. What matters more may not be improving the abilities you already have, but finding the right relationships, and the right people and things through which to build them.

What am I doing, and why? Big-company titles and startup experience look like markers of success, yet in the end they leave you stuck in the middle, neither here nor there. Wanting to get closer to stronger environments and more impressive people, hoping to find and solve problems alongside them, is at heart the wrong question. The right question is how to “seek advantage in the momentum of the situation” (求之于势), recognizing that “strategy determines everything.” First work out what you’re actually doing and what the right thing is. Only then talk about persistence.

Hamlet Syndrome and “Pragmatic Romanticism”

So how do you know what the right thing is? If the answer is just to think about it, you fall into another trap.

The course describes “Hamlet syndrome”: you know what to do, but you keep thinking and weighing and never act, until some chance scene or event forces your hand. Linger out of habit in the agony of “what should I do,” and your cognition dissolves. Most people think they can’t go far because they “don’t know enough.” But writers and philosophers suggest the answer isn’t only deeper understanding. You also need an opening, a situation.

Philosophers call this situation “thrownness” (抛入/被抛): what truly moves people to act is often an invisible force that throws them into a situation. Wang Yangming’s story shows this well. He endured public flogging, imprisonment and assassination attempts. After witnessing life and death in a wild frontier region, he found that the status and honor he had always feared losing didn’t matter that much. That is the transformation thrownness brings.

So what environment have I been in, and what has been driving me? In the US, I worried about team politics and was held back by visa problems the whole time. At ByteDance, it was basically “learn more, accumulate more,” which turned out to be just as useless. The daily worries (an unhappy boss, falling behind colleagues, getting cut) go without saying. The “hungry” and “optimizing” mindset is anti-cognitive. It makes you overlook a more fundamental question, “Is the thing itself right?”, and keeps you from focusing on where the right things are and how they come about.

So cognition is, at its core, against human nature. What makes great people great is that they can leap out of the immediate environment, the anxiety and the daily mess, and then leap back in to solve problems pragmatically. Most people are either “talkers” whose ideas sound brilliant but float in the clouds, or highly capable people with their heads down, absorbed in the pressure of the moment.

Being farsighted enough to see from above, yet grounded, pragmatic, realistic and adaptable, takes something close to a split mind. In the revolutionary era, people talked about grasping the principal contradiction. In the startup era, Zhang Yiming talks about being “pragmatic and romantic” (务实又浪漫). It may take the kind of discipline Wang Yangming described as “conquering the thief in your heart.”

As for the so-called “cutting edge of cognition”: the true source of knowledge may be the ability to dare to believe and dare to act, honed over time.

Niche Thinking

The most interesting theory in the course asks: what is the essence of cognition? The answer: a niche.

A particular kind of energy supply creates a niche, and because the niche exists, a species may emerge.

A “niche” consists of specific survival factors, an energy supply, and the ways of obtaining those resources and that energy. That combination is “cognition.” The sum of these resource supplies and the means of obtaining them forms a species. A specific niche determines a specific species, and with it a specific cognition.

Sailors carry tables of lunar phases because the moon is closely tied to their survival. The waxing and waning of the moon means nothing to someone living in a modern city, but to a sailor it’s a matter of life and death. The reason for this knowledge is simple: they occupy a specific niche. Blind people’s hearing and touch are extraordinarily sensitive, to a degree sighted people find hard to believe. That’s because sighted people don’t need hearing and touch to perceive their surroundings; they rely mainly on sight. So their touch and hearing face no selection pressure. Without selection pressure there is no evolution, and without evolution there is no such uncanny ability to perceive and survive.

A person’s level of cognition is the inverse expression of their niche.

The more outstanding a person, the more likely they occupy a distinctive niche. Mediocre people tend to be those whose niche is unclear, or who face no selection pressure. In the niche a mediocre person occupies, having or lacking a given ability has little bearing on survival. They survive regardless, so they simply go on surviving.

This perspective is illuminating. Maybe I’m not learning too slowly; maybe I’ve simply spent too long in environments without real selection pressure. Now that I’m at a startup, has that changed? All I can say is that if I wait until I’ve figured everything out before getting in the water, I may never learn to swim. So now I’m getting in first. But am I still avoiding things, inside the company or outside it? Am I facing the market realistically and pragmatically? Am I truly owning the outcomes? I’m still far from doing these well.

Next Steps in Thinking and Action

The first step all of this points to is facing the problem: seeing myself clearly and stripping away the packaging.

Who am I, really? Who are my friends? What challenges must I face? These questions open the first essay in Selected Works of Mao Zedong, and they’re the first questions I need to face too.

My generation grew up during China’s economic boom and now finds itself in a period of transition and plateau. We never lived through a real depression, but we also missed the windfall that the post-70s and post-80s generations caught. Now, as we reach middle age, the AI wave has arrived: both an opportunity and a pivotal moment. Most of us studied abroad, have big-tech experience, have seen the world, and have strong educational backgrounds. But many of us have also grown used to the system: big-tech processes, politics, the promotion game, optimizing within a given framework.

If I don’t want to spend my life stranded halfway, I need to work out where the real problems and real needs are; what I actually have now, and what I still need to acquire and leverage; whom I need to team up with; and how to move toward solving real problems.

  • Face my own and the company’s operating problems, without the packaging. I understand some business and management, and I understand technology. I know how to close deals, tell a story, and win recognition in a commercial setting. But I don’t know how to apply bigger leverage, or how to move more people and resources at scale. I know how to build something from scratch. I have truly faced situations where “nobody knows how to do this,” and pushed things to a milestone with too few resources, incomplete information, and an incomplete team. I also know how big tech turns polished details into returns. But the middle link is missing: how to creatively recombine something, use stories, ideals and culture to draw others in, and build an organization that empowers people. I haven’t yet faced that hardest problem.
  • Find more real markets and leverage, and find the competition. My strength is systematic, logical thinking: breaking problems down, defining goals, clearing bottlenecks, designing at the system level. But the truth of engineering isn’t in architecture diagrams; it’s in the implementation. Once you’ve really built a system, you know where it will break, where the trade-offs lie, when to compromise and when to hold firm. This year I’ve gained some understanding, but we’re still a scrappy outfit. And things move fast in the AI era, so I need to invest more in systematization and engineering, and learn and make an impact for real.
  • Make more time to step outside myself, and hold my strategic focus. Work on the second floor, but go up to the twentieth floor now and then, or even every day, to think and act from there. I need to turn my thinking into something I can examine from the outside: writing, projects, building things I find useful.
    • Info-AIKB-Learning-Writing suite: This grew out of my habit of reading papers. I can read papers; most people don’t. I need to set my own pace, think deeply, and turn that thinking into systems I can execute. The suite tries to answer one question: how do we rebuild the whole process of learning, knowledge management and writing with AI? Not a pile of tools, but a complete cognitive loop.
    • Personal-CRM: Where are the high-value real problems? Where is my niche? What problems can I solve better than others? What is true knowledge? Are these projects truly meaningful, or am I once again “participating” in a framework someone else defined? When it comes to the unity of knowing and doing (知行合一), a system holds me to account better than willpower alone.
