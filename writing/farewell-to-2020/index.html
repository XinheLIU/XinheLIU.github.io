Farewell to 2020, the Year the Black Swan Flew By

2025-10-29 · 11 min read Personal Growth

2020 was a year unlike so many before it, from the news media and the columns and books to everyone’s WeChat Moments. Looking back many years from now, it may gradually become as unremarkable as any other year, or even more thrilling, but for me personally I think it will most likely remain an unforgettable one.

As luck would have it, many changes in my personal life collided with the COVID pandemic sweeping the world. In January and February, before the pandemic had made its way from China to North America, the aftershocks of last year’s changes continued in my work group: of my two bosses, one had his position adjusted and the other had presumably already decided to leave, and other colleagues also left or changed roles. The new job I found in March and April ended up not being completed at the onboarding stage, hit by both COVID and the lottery, and then came several months of working from home. This period gave me more chances to settle down and do things I normally had no chance to do, but overall my productivity was somewhat lower, with gaps and waste. The second half of the year, objectively speaking, took a sharp turn downward: after my boss left in April I was working and maneuvering alone; from July, a small shoulder pain unexpectedly turned into a troublesome condition that dragged on until the end of the year; and after deciding to return to China, I spent October and November job-hunting, struggling all the way from the U.S. back to China and through quarantine, and by year-end my work was still not settled.

So all I can do is sum it up subjectively.

Achievements, Barely Found

The Big Picture

As described above, in both work and life, compared with previous years it was more a matter of holding on and flailing back and forth, with a lot of effort converted at a low rate because of various difficulties. But I think wandering, pressure, trial and error, and persistence forced me to re-examine my past experience, myself and the world; the lack of exchange with the outside world during the pandemic also provided more quiet for thinking than usual. This year for the first time I had an experience I had always wanted: my knowledge and experience took on a few rough structures and outlines, with fragments of information not only linked but also given rough positions and coordinates; most of it I do not understand or know, and many half-understood fragments, and fragments with contradictory information, acquired some boundaries.

Of course this is only the simple start of refining what I have lived through into experience, and I am far from becoming a person of real insight. But gaining experience sometimes requires purposeful, systematic learning, exchange with others, and one’s own practice, combined with repeated reflection and recording to deepen it. My own standard is that when I have a panoramic understanding of some specific problem, I can say I have a little experience; that is still some distance from being able to offer valuable insights.

Take so-called “career planning and career choice.” First, it is not a specific problem, but it is often treated as one big problem. Within this big problem, many parts can be refined into specific problems and many cannot. For example, “what kind of person, which profession and which factors combine to yield high social status, high income and fast promotion” is a relatively specific problem that can be analyzed. But “can I become such a person, and what then, and what should I do” contains more unpredictable and uncertain factors and more subjective components.

In exploring such problems, mostly one can only “take it one step at a time”: combine one’s experience, others’ views and objective data to reach a rough conclusion, and act; in acting one discovers much information and change one did not know about before, and comes back with new small questions, sometimes not even knowing what to ask. For example, in my years at Goldman Sachs, because I was in a group doing relatively innovative business, the individual and the group did entirely new work each year; the business I came in to do had basically ended by the time I left, and from group to division to firm, many people came and went. The subtleties in between—each person’s character, experience and role, and the quantifiable and subjective parts of how business value is assessed and allocated—all needed careful observation and summing up. My summaries were certainly imperfect, but I had to make do with them. Through many iterations, working out how the concepts that are virtual in university textbooks—business, personal development, management, leadership—can be spliced together with lived experience into an incomplete but reality-reflecting panorama is actually not an easy process.

Similarly, with job-hunting among US banks and funds in March and April, and, after returning to China, job-hunting domestically and working with headhunters, the processes, the experiences, and the people and companies I encountered were really just a few hours of contact each, but there are many topics to unfold from them. They range from the panorama of Chinese and American industry and the similarities and differences in how value is distributed, to cultural differences, and even future directions and room for growth, the so-called “more opportunities vs. higher starting point.” How these topics relate to me needs repeated thinking and summarizing to sort out, and what use it is to sort them out is harder still to answer.

And this is only the social side. Career choice is also tied to personal choice and growth, which is yet another line. I think that whatever the problem, one should try to “explain the profound in simple terms,” with thinking and analysis—though in the end the result may not be better than a random walk, at least the life experience is deeper.

The Road

Likewise, no amount of study, reading and principles can replace the leap of faith one has to take when problems, difficulties and choices truly land on oneself. Sometimes, even if one’s vision and goals change often, that is better than always lining up the nearby options and picking the best one as a greedy strategy—people still have to tinker a bit. With a vision and a goal, whether or not one carves out a road, trying one’s best to walk it is a way of living without regret.

Human behavior is objectively constrained by emotion, society and oneself. I hope to be able to do the following:

  • How to see the world—no one is an island; the world always has a place for oneself. When room for development and advancement is limited, look for one’s place in a larger time and space.
  • How to be a person—for myself, this means two things: optimizing my information environment and optimizing my decision models.
  • How to do things—first find what is important and meaningful to myself and to others, then look for resources; do not be an opportunist.

Persistence

Looking at the goals I set last year, some were achieved and some were not. Despite many fluctuations and difficulties, quite a few good habits carried on, such as rising early, studying, exercising, and keeping records of what I have done wherever possible. I hope to keep improving next year. Three points matter most:

  • Perfectionism is a trap; iterating repeatedly is more reliable than piling up perfection, especially for things without short-term incentives.
  • Enterprises need to accumulate data, and personal behavior also needs accumulation and data—often one does not know oneself as well as others do.
  • Plan is nothing, planning is everything. Masters of planning tend to be robust, too.

Things I Hope to Do Better

In 2021, I hope to pay more attention to the following areas.

Making Friends

This is not limited to contacting and talking with friends more often, or asking them for information or opportunities. It is more about a few aspects:

  • Having lived in many different places over the years and met many different people, I find that friends at this stage of life gradually “decohere”: lifestyles, values, pursuits, environments and so on grow more different. This is when the benefits of interaction and network effects for broadening one’s horizons really show: people on Wall Street, in Silicon Valley, in Hong Kong; engineers, product managers and securities professionals in China; working people in every industry; entrepreneurs—their differences, connections, collaboration and networks. “The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still retain the ability to function” seems increasingly needed in this age.
  • Empathy and understanding, and sincere care from the heart, are always scarce and the most valuable wealth.
  • “Look to the situation, not to blame people”—often it is through people that one sees the situation; caring about others is caring about oneself.

Managing Change

Some things, like returning to China, were still full of variables and deviations in execution even after I had done the so-called preparations. Of course this was not only because of this year’s special black swan, but more because of a shortfall in the skill of adapting on the fly. Staff changes, the external environment, illness—these cannot all be foreseen and prepared for, but I should handle it better than I do now if it happens again. “The orthodox and the unorthodox give birth to each other”—the longer one lives, the more this ability should grow. In future I still need to summarize and study more.

Learning

This year during the pandemic I took many courses and read quite a few books that I usually had not settled down to read. The experience was good, but looking back, much of it repeated what I already knew, and the efficiency of acquiring new knowledge was not that high. I hope to do better going forward in these ways:

  • Be result-oriented. For example, I set a flag last year to write, but could not follow through, because writing only for myself turned out not to be a good plan; writing should have a purpose.
  • Be selective. Choose vertical directions where possible, reduce repeated learning, and take one topic to the extreme and go deeper, forming a T-shaped knowledge structure.
  • Fewer but better, systematic learning rather than piling up content. For example, the several courses I took during the pandemic were far less valuable than the less than two weeks I spent after returning to China carefully sorting out the structure of the relevant papers.

Farewell to the special year of 2020. We are all survivors and thrivers; I wish every kind person greater strength.

December 31, 2020

In Lanzhou
