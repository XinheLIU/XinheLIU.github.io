Returning to China and the Job Search: A Retrospective

2025-11-03 · 16 min read Personal Growth

Quite a few friends have recently been asking about returning to China and building a career here, and are curious about why I came back. I returned from the U.S. at the end of last year and spent about three months, on and off, talking to employers. Now that I have been at my new job for about a month, I want to look back on my return and the job search, and share my thinking on returning, my plans, and some reflections from the job search.

Why I Chose to Return

First, what the reasons are not.

Before returning I did hit some obstacles in work and life, and the pandemic in the U.S. was indeed severe; but none of these constituted a reason I had to leave or a difficulty that could not be solved. Whether the pandemic, visas or work problems, they were all relatively temporary; I also have quite a few friends who met the same difficulties but stuck to their original path and are doing better and better.

Even in the long run, I do not think the U.S. will go downhill from here, nor that developing a career in the U.S. is worse than in China. This is a big question that has to be discussed from different sides; in many of the areas I have come into contact with, the U.S. is still ahead, developing fast and full of vitality. People often overestimate short-term change and underestimate long-term change, and the pandemic’s impact on the U.S. was actually the smallest in the world (per IMF data); it is the rest of the world that brought more uncertainty. Even if in some aspects and some industries the U.S. is really declining slowly, for an individual, different environments may offer different opportunities and troubles, and one cannot generalize. So doing something with a flavor of speculation does not necessarily bring any reward.

The reasons I considered returning are mainly two.

Accelerating Change

Whether from rational analysis or emotional experience, the pandemic of 2020 (like the financial crisis of 2008) will produce some long-term or even permanent changes at every scale, from global development down to personal plans. These changes will on one hand accelerate and intensify existing trends or contradictions, and on the other produce some new structural changes. Many big changes are already in the daily news—the gap between rich and poor, globalization, and regional geopolitical cooperation are shifting faster; and friends who work must also feel that companies are making structural changes in business operations, the virtual economy, industrial intelligence and technology R&D.

For an individual, long-term prediction is hard, and the visible change is mainly greater uncertainty. Under accelerating change, the certainty of some conditions—for example, learning and exploring in a stable work environment and a globalized platform, or building technologies and products with broad applicability—has weakened. The scarcity of some options and the standards for measuring risk may also differ from before—regional cooperation in business will be relatively strengthened, and the costs and rewards of different choices will also become more polarized; these changes may have a fairly large impact on an individual’s long-term plans. For an individual, this means, on one hand, a higher requirement to make progress faster than the speed of change, and on the other, a higher requirement for tolerance of uncertainty and risk. On both dimensions, there is indeed a little more room to maneuver in China than in the U.S.

Opportunity and Cost of Trial and Error

Objectively, right now is not a good time to return—two or three years of experience is not enough to be an expert in a direction, and many people returned during the pandemic for various reasons, so scarcity is also poor. In this case the decisive factor is really one’s own situation and preferences. Simply put, even in the U.S. I faced the question of changing jobs or even changing careers, for reasons I will expand on below. But this is a decision strongly tied to personal circumstances. Relatively speaking, returning to China reduces some uncertainty and offers more options, which is probably what I most urgently need over the next year or two.

But let me add that I do not believe that so-called “keeping more options” or “tinkering” necessarily brings real value. Statistically, most people I know still obtained higher degrees, income and positions by working hard plus being “excellent,” whether in grades, looks or a particular skill. Choice is of course crucial here, but it is not the case that having more chances to choose, or being more willing to tinker, makes one choose better. For example, in investing, excess returns come from more information or lower cost; going deep in one field is not necessarily worse than switching often. In terms of income, career development, quality of life and stability alone, per capita GDP in China’s first-tier cities is currently only about half that of the U.S.

The Job Search

Before April 2020 I applied to a batch of jobs in the U.S., but after losing the H-1B lottery for the third time in April, by June I had basically made up my mind to return to China, and started gathering information and talking with people I knew. July and August were delayed by an injury, and in September, after getting a batch of offers, I felt I had not thought many things through or understood them well, and decided instead to go back to China and look further. After returning, most of the offers came in the two months from December to January, with some rest time too, adding up to about half a year. Of course, in between I adjusted my plans and execution step by step, and most of the time was in fact “seeing as I went,” gaining knowledge mainly through practice. For job seekers with a clear direction, concentrating it into two to four months should be reasonable.

In fact, job hunting is a process with great information asymmetry, so the probability of making the optimal choice is very low. For someone with two or three years of experience, with some path dependence and not a domain expert, and given that in China, compared with abroad, roles that are clearly divided by function and precisely matched to one’s background are far fewer and companies develop and change faster, this asymmetry is especially pronounced. I think a good mindset is to treat the job search as a decision-making exercise—making evidence-based decisions and reviewing them matters more than being sure to make the right one. Looking back, there is still a great deal I could have improved in this process.

Listing the Options

The first step in finding a job is to increase your options as much as possible and gather the necessary information—given my background, I tried to reach out in finance (banks, brokerages, funds, private equity) and the internet (fintech, traditional companies, PE/VC-type firms, big and small companies); but much information was learned only in the course of contact, and this process in fact takes a long time and is not necessarily very efficient. Some friends around me, especially professionals, sometimes skip this step or blindly assume that “applying wherever I can” counts as expanding options.

It is in fact necessary to spend time organizing and improving your sources of information. In the job search, referrals among acquaintances are the most efficient channel (I have also passed the better options I found to a friend who wanted to return to China, and he is about to start); alumni associations and indirect acquaintances come next; in addition, for new businesses at some companies, relatively internal information or information from social networks can be approached on your own; and by the time one reaches public postings and headhunters, there are probably only routine hiring, positions that could not be filled internally, and new business expansion—opportunities for “hiring strangers.” And this is even more severe in less developed markets. As for résumés, precisely because the opportunities that require résumé screening have very clear requirements, a résumé that is too generic is worse than not applying.

In my own search, the way I allocated effort across these information tiers was inversely proportional to their effectiveness, and I overlooked a lot of effective information. And because I did not define my circle of competence clearly, I spent too much time screening opportunities and revising résumés (I applied to over 40 opportunities and revised my résumé about 20 times), which I should reflect on.

Evaluating the Options

How many choices you get mostly depends on timing and external factors, but how you weigh the value of each option directly reflects your level of cognition. Many people succeed because of momentary luck, but those who make the right choices over the long term usually do so because of an advantage in information and cognition.

In most cases, one’s level of cognition is still proportional to the amount of information one holds. Many of the most critical choices in life—choosing a university and major with almost no systematic understanding of majors and the job market; choosing a career direction knowing nothing about how the business world works; choosing a partner with limited understanding of oneself and of relationships—involve very little effective information, and people choosing often draw on the experience of elders or others, and of course rely more on luck. But the later one goes, although most people’s chances to choose get fewer, those with more information often have more chances to choose, and after choosing right can move up again, forming a virtuous cycle; so improving one’s cognition as needed and being prepared in case opportunity comes is still necessary.

In this job search, precisely because I had plenty of time, I gathered a lot of information, but I did not do well at thinking about how to integrate it into a framework for evaluating each option. For example, my final switch from finance to the internet was based on some big pieces of information—such as that China, the virtual economy and the technology industry are developing well, and that my relative advantage and path lie at the intersection of technology and business; I ended up choosing data because it seemed that I could do some innovative work if things went well, and, if not, could increase my scarcity a bit through cross-disciplinary skills (the so-called “two 25% theory”); some understanding of my own micro-level motives—such as wanting a job with more collaboration and more expression; and some more specific information—pay, location, the people who work there every day, and so on. But which of this information was most critical, how the pieces relate and how they are weighted, and how much they are worth to me, I did not think through.

Another thing I did not do well was a lack of deep thinking. Simply put, if I am going to take a job in the end, I should have at least a customer-service-level understanding of the products or technology I will work on. For the industry I enter, I should be able to draw a panorama. And all of this can be done with public information.

Evidence-Based Decisions

Making decisions through a decision framework, with simple criteria and principles, is better than going purely by feel. This is one of the parts I did relatively well this time, and I accumulated a few takeaways.

Correct Cognitive Biases

Distinguishing “feeling” from “thinking,” and the subjective from the objective, is actually very hard. So it is necessary to correct one’s cognitive biases as much as possible, including emotion, overvaluing short-term information, and risk aversion. A good solution is to step back, think carefully, and use some tools to analyze. These could be purely logical tools, such as the “four causes”; or one can gather more data to verify. Take interviews: I recorded the specific information obtained in interviews, including the long-term career development of most people at the company, and found that the effective information I got was actually very little. And the most effective information may be the interviewer’s own career development and working state, plus some external sources (such as studies and surveys), which together give a rough estimate of what I would probably look like in a few years if I joined. As for how a specific interview felt, and the views rather than facts the interviewer revealed, one must make one’s own judgment.

Try to Have a Chance at Trial and Error

Try to treat the interview process as a pilot run. Of course in many cases this is hard, but at higher-quality large companies one can still see some things. If you can talk directly with insiders or attend some talks, they can provide similar information. This time a small company gave me the chance to visit the workplace first, which was very valuable. Of course, this kind of information needs your own standards for judgment; for example, most talks say that in the U.S. the starting point is relatively high but growth is slow, while in China the starting point is low but development is fast, and many people are already running funds or serving as CEOs at a young age. These facts are fine, but they may just be observer bias or Berkson’s paradox.

On Risk and Return

Risk—the essence of decision-making is still risk and return. And my biggest takeaway is that very often one has to consider the measurability of risk as well as the risk itself. For example, for a career change, in the short term or emotionally it seems scary to give up a considerable existing stock and tinker blindly, but in the long run, even if one or two years are spent exploring an unrelated field, the risk of failure is not entirely uncontrollable. Another example is that the information asymmetry at large companies is much better, and at famous companies the possible unfavorable situations are relatively knowable. At small companies it depends on how well you know the field and how much you trust it. Each person’s risk preference is related, on one hand, to their specific circumstances (age, family, and so on) and, on the other, to personality, and the former is more measurable.

Return—the most important thing is “knowing what you want.” More important than sources of information are cognition, self-understanding and planning. On one hand, the clearer your needs, the easier the step of looking for potential options; sometimes the more one can focus on one’s own micro-level motives and specific needs, the more one can dig out, with direction, opportunities that are hard to find from an external orientation alone. On the other hand, external information changes quickly, while one’s own preferences may change relatively slowly. When you are very clear about the ranking of your needs, decisions are also made much more efficiently.

Action Matters More Than Planning

Having said all this, no matter how perfect the plan beforehand, all kinds of surprises still come up in execution. More critical than strategizing is the ability to adapt on the fly. I still have a lot of room to improve in being action-oriented; in real development there is never a case in which everything goes smoothly according to the original idea. And much information and knowledge is exactly what cannot be obtained without trying it personally. To make progress in knowing, one can only work harder in doing. Whether one has a long-term plan or takes it one step at a time, the real test often comes after execution begins.

May friends who strive for their lives gain personal growth and spiritual fulfillment.

April 2021, in Shanghai
